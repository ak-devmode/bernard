## 2.2 Priority 2: Member Retention and Onboarding Codification

Retention to date has been driven by Alex's personal engagement. This will not scale. As of February 2026, Gita has taken over onboarding and doesn't have the full backstory on every member. This section defines the onboarding playbook and the ongoing engagement approach.

### 2.2.1 The Peace-of-Mind Tension

A core insight about this business: the most powerful value Padma Care delivers — the peace of mind that someone is there when you need them — is also the hardest thing to sell and the most fragile thing to retain. Members have explicitly said that knowing Padma is there has real value, independent of how often they use the service. This means engagement cannot look like pushing appointments or upselling healthcare. Members who feel sold to will leave faster than members who feel underserved. Every touchpoint must deliver genuine value or useful information without creating a transaction expectation. The onboarding sequence below is designed with this principle: each step says "we're getting to know you so we can serve you better when the time comes" — not "here's what you should buy next."

### 2.2.2 Onboarding Sequence

**Week 1: Welcome + PHA Introduction + Pending Concerns**

2.2.2.1 Welcome email: articulates all pieces of the program in one place, provides key contact information including email address. This is the single reference document for "what did I sign up for."

2.2.2.2 PHA personal introduction via WhatsApp: save my number, brief personal intro, and one specific micro-prompt — "What's one health question you've been sitting on?" This immediately demonstrates the service in action rather than just introducing a contact.

2.2.2.3 Address any pending concerns or items raised during the signup process (captured in HubSpot notes or signup form). PHA focuses on these first before moving to standard onboarding. This signals responsiveness and personal attention from day one.

**Week 2: Emergency Health Directive ("Day One Kit")**

2.2.2.4 Deliver a personalized emergency health directive based on the member's area of Bali. Contents: which ER to go to for their location, ambulance number, 4-5 do's and don'ts for emergency situations, and the closing line "if you're unsure, text us first — that's what we're here for."

2.2.2.5 This is genuinely useful, localized (not available via Google), and creates an early "I already got value from this" moment. It's a level above prospect-stage conversation but generic enough to produce efficiently. Reinforces the habit of reaching out to Padma first.

**Day 30: Medical History via Voice Notes**

2.2.2.6 Capture a patient medical history through WhatsApp voice notes instead of traditional forms. Traditional medical history forms are designed for clinicians, not patients — they produce bad data (the "tick no on 300 boxes" problem) and patients with complex histories either give up or produce unstructured dumps.

2.2.2.7 Execution: PHA sends a personal voice note — "Hey [name], we'd love to get a quick health snapshot so we can serve you better — just reply to these questions whenever you have a minute, voice notes are perfect." Then deliver 5-6 conversational prompts via WhatsApp (not a form to narrate against): "Tell us about any medications you take regularly," "Any surgeries or hospital stays in the past?" "Any conditions you manage ongoing — diabetes, blood pressure, allergies?" "Anything else you think your doctor should know?"

2.2.2.8 For the healthy person with nothing to report, this takes 60 seconds. For the person with a complex history, they narrate naturally and the important information comes out in context rather than buried in checkboxes.

2.2.2.9 AI processing step: transcribe voice notes, pass through a structured extraction prompt mapped to internal medical history categories, flag anything requiring clinical follow-up, produce a clean internal document. Clinical team member reviews output before it goes into the member file (5-minute review instead of 30-minute data entry). Thesis — needs to be built and tested, but technically straightforward.

**Ongoing: Non-Transactional Engagement**

2.2.2.10 Monthly newsletter (see Section 5.4) is the primary ongoing touchpoint for all members.

2.2.2.11 Seasonal and situational value touches — when relevant, PHA sends genuinely useful non-salesy information: seasonal health alerts ("dengue cases are up in your area"), relevant network updates ("we just added a great pediatric dentist"), or area-specific advisories. These demonstrate that Padma is paying attention without pushing toward an appointment.

2.2.2.12 The first appointment, when it happens organically, includes a complimentary upgrade to demonstrate the quality of access Padma provides. This cannot be forced or scheduled — it happens when the member naturally needs care. If a member reaches Day 90+ without any service interaction, the PHA may reach out with a seasonal health note or relevant update, but never with a prompt to book an appointment.

2.2.3.2 Deliverable: a documented playbook covering the sequence above, PHA responsibilities at each step, templates for the welcome email and emergency directive, the voice note prompt sequence, and the AI processing workflow for medical histories

# 3. Service Definition

## 3.1 Current Positioning Statement

Padma Care is a membership-based insider healthcare program for Bali expats. We connect members to top doctors, negotiate pricing on their behalf, and provide on-demand guidance for health concerns and questions. Quality healthcare in Bali is fragmented and hard to navigate — Padma Care coordinates access to the best local care, and when local options fall short, we're the first to tell you and help arrange treatment offshore.

## 3.2 One-Liner (Value Proposition)

Padma Care provides end-to-end medical support so members never have to navigate the healthcare system alone.

Note on positioning: the deepest value Padma delivers is peace of mind — knowing someone competent is there when you need them. Members have explicitly said this. But peace of mind is intangible and nearly impossible to sell directly. The messaging strategy therefore leads with tangible benefits (vetted doctors, negotiated pricing, navigation support) that make the membership feel like a no-brainer, while the peace of mind compounds quietly over time as the real retention driver. The 350,000 IDR price point is deliberately set low enough to enable a "sure, why not" decision rather than a "let me think about it" — the price is a conversion tool, not a revenue optimization.

## 3.3 Two-Track Service Model

Padma Care delivers two distinct types of value that correspond to two different states of need. We do not use the clinical terms "outpatient" and "inpatient" in marketing because prospects have no frame of reference for these distinctions. Instead, we frame around the experience.

### 3.3.1 Concierge — Everyday Healthcare Navigation

This is the core membership service. It covers routine, planned, and preventive healthcare — the situations where you need a good doctor, fair pricing, and someone who knows the system.

3.3.1.1 What it includes: personal health advisor (PHA), access to 108 vetted specialists, negotiated hospital pricing (local rates regardless of immigration status), on-demand guidance for health questions, appointment coordination, insurance liaison, preventive care planning

3.3.1.2 Who it's for: families and individuals who want to set up healthcare properly and have ongoing support for everyday medical needs

3.3.1.3 Emotional frame: confidence, preparedness, insider access — "I know exactly who to call and where to go"

3.3.1.4 Primary target: relocating families (proactive), expat residents (proactive or post-experience)

### 3.3.2 Advocacy — Hospital Care Support

This is for when someone is admitted to hospital and the experience becomes overwhelming, confusing, or feels uncontrolled. The core value is bridging the gap between what a patient expects from a hospital stay and what Indonesian hospitals actually deliver by default.

3.3.2.1 What it includes: daily care coordination (250,000 IDR/day base), treatment monitoring and updates, options and second opinions, bill vetting and pricing advocacy, expectation management (what is normal vs. what is a concern), clinical documentation (managed care reports — nothing like this exists anywhere else in Indonesia)

3.3.2.2 Optional add-ons: 24/7 nursing care, emergency first-world second opinions, quality-of-life services (menu still being developed)

3.3.2.3 Who it's for: anyone currently admitted to a hospital in Bali who feels lost, confused, or unsupported — regardless of existing membership status

3.3.2.4 Emotional frame: not "crisis management" and not "peace of mind" — advocacy patients are not experiencing peace. They are experiencing loss of control, confusion, and often fear or anger. The value Padma brings is not comfort — it's clarity. Clarity restores agency. Once someone understands what's happening and what their options are, they come back to earth on their own terms. We don't manage their emotions; we give them the information to make decisions.

3.3.2.5 Core messaging insight: "The healthcare system here wasn't built for us." This single line does all the work. It names the structural gap (not the hospital's fault, not the patient's fault — a systemic reality). It puts Padma and the patient on the same team ("us" not "you"). It's non-adversarial toward hospitals and doctors (the system was optimized for efficiency, which is a reasonable goal — it just wasn't optimized for the foreign patient's experience of being inside it). And it allows every patient to project their own specific frustration onto it — whether that's communication, transparency, pricing, pace, or all of it.

3.3.2.6 Messaging territories to test (in real advocacy cases — let patient reactions guide which lands):

- **Territory A: "The healthcare system in Indonesia wasn't built for us. Padma is."** The tightest version. Sets up the problem and positions Padma as the answer in five words. Doesn't name emotions, doesn't promise outcomes, doesn't claim to fix anything. Says: there's a gap, and we exist to fill it. Use The healthcare system **here** wasn't built for us in SM marketing.

- **Territory B: "You shouldn't have to figure out a healthcare system while you're inside it."** Names the absurdity of the situation. Immediately recognizable to anyone who's been there — you're supposed to be recovering, but instead you're trying to decode how things work. Implies Padma takes the "figuring out" off your plate. Also works for the family member doing the worrying and decision-making from outside the room.

3.3.2.7 Anchor video concept (advocacy brand piece — produce when ready):

ICU ward. EKG beeping. Stillness. Voiceover: "The hospital system here wasn't built for us." Pause. Then a montage of fast-paced cuts — long BPJS waiting line, hectic ER, operating theatre, pharmacy pills, doctors rushing, Indonesian doctor (with mask on) speaking to a confused Western patient in a hospital bed, crowded group recovery room with nurses walking by. families by each bed, physiotherapy on dilapitated looking equipment, another ER scene. Voiceover over montage: "The system here was optimized for efficiency. Not for you being in it." Back to the ICU. Beeping. Still there. But the pause after the storm now feels different — steady, reassuring. The beeping that felt ominous at the start now feels like "still here." Padma Care Patient Advocacy appears on screen. Voiceover: "Padma Care patient advocacy helps 500 patients per year make Indonesian healthcare feel like it was built for them." Contact information.

The video mirrors the actual patient emotional arc: stuck in stillness → chaos of the system → return to stillness, but now with context and company. Nothing changed medically between the opening and closing shot. What changed is the feeling of being alone in it.

Note: "500 patients per year" is credible — Crew Care (Padma's managed care line for overseas workers) handles this volume annually in exactly this context: managing surgical intervention and post-operative coordination for inpatients. This operational foundation is what makes Padma uniquely qualified for expat patient advocacy. The stat can be used as-is; the Crew Care backstory adds depth when needed (e.g., in the video companion content or doctor-facing pitch) but the number stands on its own.

3.3.2.8 Conversion path: advocacy patients are not pitched for concierge membership during their hospital stay. After discharge, the concierge value proposition is introduced — anchored in the negotiated pricing benefit ("if this situation were to happen again, our members save significantly — we estimate [x] million IDR in savings on a case like yours, that's [y] years of membership right there just in case you need us again").

## 3.4 Service Fundamentals

3.4.1 Monthly membership: 350,000 IDR (~$24 USD) + a la carte options

3.4.2 Annual prepaid option available (with or without MCU) — low uptake to date (1 buyer)

3.4.3 Negotiated pricing at 7 out of 11 preferred hospitals — local rates regardless of immigration status

3.4.4 Curated network of 108 vetted doctors from 5,000+ practicing in Bali

3.4.5 40-minute free consultation for prospects

3.4.6 Insurance is NOT a requirement (corrected from v1) — service supplements insurance where it exists, and provides standalone value where it doesn't (particularly relevant for retirees 65+)

## 3.5 Competitive Landscape

3.5.1 No direct competitor offers the same combination of care coordination, pricing advocacy, vetted specialist network, and clinical documentation

3.5.2 BaliDoc (balidoc.com) is the nearest comparable — a telehealth platform founded 2023, targeting tourists/expats/nomads with online consultations ($150/month subscription), prescription management, and medication delivery. Key differences: BaliDoc is telemedicine-first with 2 GPs; Padma Care is relationship-based navigation with 108 specialists across all disciplines. BaliDoc explicitly does not handle emergencies or advanced diagnostics. BaliDoc is also partnering with Our Year in Bali (OYIB) on pre-departure health consultations — this is a competitive overlap worth monitoring

3.5.3 Informal competitors: former expat nurses/doctors offering ad-hoc healthcare navigation — none have organized business models, consistent service delivery, or the ability to reliably collect payment. People don't instinctively expect to pay for navigation help

3.5.4 Adjacent services (not direct competitors): home care providers, home lab testing, home IV services, hospitality/visitor home care, hospital-based private nurses, insurance concierge services. Market is fragmented with no organized player

3.5.5 Key differentiators: flat monthly rate (vs. hourly billing), negotiated hospital pricing, independence from any hospital group, managed care documentation (unique in Indonesia), 17-year operational history through Padma Medical Group

# 4. Target Audiences

## 4.1 Segment 1: Relocating Families (Primary)

### 4.1.1 Profile

Families relocating to Bali, primarily European/North American. Typically have children, are in planning/checklist-completion mode, and are making multiple setup decisions simultaneously (school, housing, visa, healthcare). Eventually extend targeting to all nationalities and wealthy Indonesians — current messaging is optimized for Western expat experience.

### 4.1.2 Lead Sources (Priority Order)

4.1.2.1 Our Year in Bali partnership (primary driver — see Section 5)

4.1.2.2 SEO / AI Overview surfacing (second priority — see Section 6)

4.1.2.3 Other channels to be identified (thesis — what other touchpoints exist for families actively planning a Bali relocation? Online communities, relocation agencies, international school networks, visa agents?)

### 4.1.3 Conversion Psychology

4.1.3.1 Optimal conversion window: T-60 to T-30 (days before arrival). Average time from first contact to close is approximately 1 week when contacted within this window.

4.1.3.2 If not converted by T-7, close rate drops approximately 80%.

4.1.3.3 Leads arriving earlier than T-90 go into holding pattern (newsletter / active prospect list).

4.1.3.4 OYIB generally delivers leads between T-150 and T-30, almost never closer than T-30.

4.1.3.5 What we were wrong about (v1): 2-3 weeks post-move is the wrong time to reach relocators. By then they are overwhelmed and no longer in planning mode. Must engage before arrival.

### 4.1.4 Prospect Journey Touchpoints

T-90+: Holding pattern — newsletter, educational content, stay warm
T-90: Active nurture begins
T-60: Sweetspot entry — content intensifies, consultation offered
T-30: Decision urgency — "this is the last thing on the checklist"
T-7: Final pre-arrival touch
T+7: Welcome / post-arrival check-in (if not yet converted, conversion probability significantly reduced)
T+30: Last reasonable touch before moving to long-term nurture

Note: "Months" are guideposts used in prospect-facing communications. Internal tracking uses days-to-arrival.

### 4.1.5 Messaging Framework (Relocators)

4.1.5.1 Core reframe: NOT "insurance for when something goes wrong" — BUT "the healthcare setup every prepared family has"

4.1.5.2 The psychological shift: it's not about if you'll need healthcare in Bali. It's about how you'll handle healthcare in Bali. Every family needs doctors eventually — the question is whether you figure it out stressed or set it up while thinking clearly.

4.1.5.3 Key messaging reframes:

| Avoid | Use Instead |
|-------|-------------|
| "Medical concierge" (without unpacking it) | "Healthcare advocate" / "Your insider healthcare network" |
| "When you need us" | "From day one, here's how it works" |
| "Insurance for emergencies" | "Essential infrastructure for life in Bali" |
| "We rescue families" | "We help smart families set up properly" |
| "Premium service" | "Part of your Bali checklist" |
| "Crisis intervention" | "Preventive setup + ongoing support" |

4.1.5.4 Nurture content themes by timing:

- T-90 to T-60: Checklist positioning — "one thing most families forget." Healthcare as natural part of relocation planning alongside school, housing, visa.
- T-60 to T-30: Insider knowledge — pricing reality (passport-based pricing), the 108 vs. 5,000 doctor stat, the decision paralysis problem. Conspiratorial/FOMO tone: "here's what they won't tell you."
- T-30 to T-7: Healthy urgency — approaching move date, chaos avoidance, friction removal ("10 minutes, then it's checked off"). Dual CTA: direct signup or free consultation.
- T+7: Welcome touch, no guilt if not converted, door open.

### 4.1.6 Current Nurture Execution

Nurture sequence is manually executed and should stay manual for now — enables iteration on message, medium (WhatsApp primary, email secondary), and timing. Automated only after the playbook is validated.

### 4.1.7 Conversion Inertia

The 80% consultation "close rate" is misleading. In practice, approximately 30% of consultation attendees convert immediately. 50% convert eventually after the call. The remaining 15% say "wow, this is amazing" and then fall out before completing signup — not because they object, but because of inertia. They're sold on the concept but haven't felt enough friction in their lives yet to overcome the small friction of completing the signup. This is the core conversion challenge: selling peace of mind to people who feel fine right now.

4.1.7.1 What works: "Let's do it together right now while I'm on the phone" — eliminating the gap between intent and action. "It only takes 10 minutes and then you're live." "Pick any start date you want."

4.1.7.2 What doesn't work: pushing to a follow-up consultation, giving them time to "think about it," or adding steps between intent and signup.

4.1.7.3 98 out of 100 prospects say "this is a great service, so needed" — the product-market fit signal is strong. The conversion problem is mechanical (inertia and timing), not conceptual (objection to the service). Marketing should focus on making the value so tangible and the signup so frictionless that the "sure, why not" threshold is easily crossed.

## 4.2 Segment 2: Expat Residents (Secondary)

### 4.2.1 Profile

Established expats already living in Bali. Historically the hardest segment to convert.

### 4.2.2 Conversion Challenge

4.2.2.1 Under normal conditions: "I'm healthy, why do I need to add a cost?"

4.2.2.2 Right after a negative hospital experience: messaging feels like ambulance chasing

4.2.2.3 10 days after incident: dissatisfaction/anger has faded and urgency is gone

4.2.2.4 Decision window for reactive conversion: 72 hours to ~10 days post-negative experience (narrower than v1 estimate)

### 4.2.3 Revised Conversion Strategy

The primary unlock for residents is to reach them while they are in hospital — this is where the advocacy service becomes the entry point.

4.2.3.1 Funnel: negative hospital experience → advocacy sign-up (immediate value) → satisfaction with advocacy service → convert to concierge membership post-discharge

4.2.3.2 Channels to reach in-hospital prospects:

- Social media messaging targeting frustrated inpatients (thesis — needs testing; see 6.2.4)
- Doctor referral program (primary channel — see Section 4)
- SM groups where expats discuss hospital experiences in real time

4.2.3.3 For residents not currently in hospital, the approach is brand awareness and trust-building through content, online presence maturity, and word-of-mouth/referral program

### 4.2.4 SM Targeting of In-Hospital Prospects

Direction: reach people currently in hospital whose experience isn't meeting their expectations. Messaging should use the "system wasn't built for us" framework (see Section 3.3.2.5) rather than naming frustration or negative emotions. This threads the needle: it acknowledges the patient's experience without criticizing the hospital or doctor, and positions Padma as filling a structural gap rather than rescuing from a failure.

Example directions for SM (all need testing): "The healthcare system here wasn't built for us. We were." or "You shouldn't have to figure out a healthcare system while you're inside it." These work as ad copy and as organic post hooks.

Open question: the doctor referral channel (Section 6) is likely far more effective than SM for reaching in-hospital patients, because the recommendation comes from a trusted authority at the point of need. SM-to-inpatient may prove to be a low-conversion channel relative to effort. Prioritize doctor referrals; treat SM as supplementary.

## 4.3 Segment 3: Retirees

### 4.3.1 Profile

Retirees living in or relocating to Bali. Distinct characteristics: more cost-conscious (fixed income), less likely to consume social media, cannot obtain insurance over age 65, different healthcare needs (chronic conditions, more frequent care interactions).

### 4.3.2 Positioning

Not positioned as "insurance alternative" — more as "what to do when insurance isn't an option." Core value: navigating the system, vetting bills, negotiated pricing so they don't get overcharged, and having someone who understands their needs.

Open gap: hospice planning / end-of-life care support. We don't currently have a good solution for this and it's a real need in this segment.

8.3.3.2 Partnership with Retire in Bali Facebook group admins — initial contact through Carmen at Retire in Bali

8.3.3.3 Online info session (first step) — purpose is to surface concerns and tailor subsequent event content, not to sell

8.3.3.4 Retirees may fall into either concierge or advocacy track depending on their current state

## 4.4 Exclusions

4.4.1 Digital nomads — low conversion, transient, not the right fit

4.4.2 Severely ill patients seeking individual companions — prefer dedicated care arrangements over membership model

## 4.5 Core Pain Points (All Segments — Priority Order)

# 6. Inpatient Advocacy Program (Doctor Referral)

## 6.1 Program Overview

Recruit hospital doctors to refer inpatients to Padma's advocacy service. Doctors are incentivized financially and the pitch is positioned as complementary — Padma fills the gap between patient expectations and hospital reality, making the doctor's life easier without taking any revenue from the hospital or doctor.

## 6.2 Pitch to Doctors

6.2.1 We help explain to inpatients what is a reasonable expectation for their hospital stay — what is possible and what is not possible by default

6.2.2 We bridge the gaps between patient expectations and reality with our services

6.2.3 We don't take any profit away from the hospital or doctor — we make everyone's life easier

6.2.4 We provide comprehensive clinical documentation (the managed care report is the proof point — nothing like it exists anywhere in Indonesia)

6.2.5 Financial incentive: 2,000,000 IDR per patient referred who signs up

6.2.6 Bonus: if a referring doctor recruits another doctor who then refers a patient, the original doctor receives an additional 1,000,000 IDR for that doctor's first patient

## 6.3 Pitch to Patients (Inpatient Prospects)

6.3.1 Messaging direction: you don't have to navigate this alone. We understand what you're going through, we know this system, and we will make sure you understand your options at every step.

6.3.2 No minimum commitment — Padma absorbs the risk if a patient signs up for only a day or two

6.3.3 Baseline service (250,000 IDR/day): coordination, answering questions, staying abreast of treatment, providing updates, giving options, advocating on the patient's behalf (second opinions, treatment vetting, pricing review)

6.3.4 Add-on menu: 24/7 nursing care, emergency first-world second opinions, other quality-of-life services (menu still being developed)

## 6.4 Economics

6.4.1 Typical advocacy case: 3 days coordination @ 250,000 + 2 nurse shift @ 800,000 + 2 follow-up appointment @ 800,000 = approximately 4,550,000 IDR per case + conversion to concierge membership

6.4.2 Upside cases exist: recent complex case generated 95,000,000 IDR total revenue with 42,000,000 IDR profit — this is an outlier but demonstrates the ceiling

6.4.3 Average target advocacy revenue per case: approximately 5,000,000 IDR

6.4.4 Doctor referral cost (2,000,000 IDR) is economically viable against average case revenue

6.4.5 Post-advocacy conversion to concierge membership is expected to be very high 

## 6.5 Rollout Plan

6.5.1 Collateral needed (dependencies for socialization): reference managed care report (complete), 1-pager for doctors (pending — Alex), 1-pager for patients / digital (pending — Alex), referral agreement (not urgent)

6.5.2 Gita to begin socializing with Padma's 108 specialist doctors first (managed care network), then drill down by specialty

6.5.3 Feedback loop: Gita captures doctor objections, enthusiasm, specific language that resonates, and no-go areas during socialization — this early intel feeds back into messaging and materials refinement

6.5.4 Sequence: specialist doctor outreach → refine pitch based on feedback → expand to broader hospital relationships → iterate

## 6.6 Quality Assurance

6.6.1 Alex personally involved in all early advocacy cases — QA at this stage requires founder-level pattern recognition and cannot be delegated

6.6.2 Specialist scorecard: Gita building a framework to evaluate doctors in the network. Minimum 1 check-in per year per doctor to evaluate against the scorecard.

6.6.3 Strike policy: 3-strike policy across all services (Crew Care + Padma Care combined), but 1-strike policy for Padma Care specifically — one negative service experience and we stop referring until the next evaluation cycle

6.6.4 Risk management for doctor referral program: if a referred patient has a bad experience with Padma's advocacy, that doctor is unlikely to refer again and may actively discourage colleagues. First 10 cases must be excellent — set clear internal expectations about service level before scaling the referral network.

## 6.7 Strategic Framing

The advocacy program is a business line, not just a marketing play. The two lines (concierge and advocacy) are symbiotic — advocacy creates the entry point for the residential market where other access points haven't worked, and satisfied advocacy patients convert to concierge members. But advocacy also generates meaningful standalone revenue (average 5,000,000 IDR per case, with significant upside on complex cases). Building a reputation as a good patient advocate is a business by itself.

8.1.3.4 Ensure content is factually specific, includes real data points (the 108/5,000 stat, pricing examples, hospital names), and demonstrates first-hand expertise
