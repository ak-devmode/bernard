## 1. What This Call Is

A free, 10-minute inbound call from a patient or (more commonly) their family member who has received the Padma Care advocacy 1-pager — either from a hospital doctor, staff member, or via WhatsApp forward. They are in a hospital situation right now. They are stressed, possibly scared, and looking for someone who sounds like they know what's going on.

This call is not a sales pitch. It is a demonstration of competence. The call itself is the product sample — if we do it well, the engagement follows naturally.

---

## 2. Before the Call

### 2.1 Information You Need

If possible before calling back or picking up, check:
- Which hospital? (Determines which doctors/systems we know)
- Patient or family member calling? (Affects how you pitch information)
- Any context from the referring doctor? (Sometimes we get a heads-up)

Often you'll have none of this. That's fine — the call structure handles discovery.

### 2.2 Have Ready

- The advocacy program PDF (to send via WhatsApp immediately after the call)
- WhatsApp open on desktop for fast follow-up
- A calm, unhurried tone — even if you're busy. This person needs to feel like you have time for them

## 3. The Call — Five Beats

The call has five beats. They don't need to happen in rigid order, but the shape should be consistent. Total target: 10 minutes. It can go longer if the caller needs it, but the structure below keeps it focused.

### Beat 1 — Open and Orient (1-2 minutes)

**Goal:** Establish who's calling, what's happening, and who the patient is.

Open warm, not clinical:

> "Hi, this is [name] from Padma Care. How can I help?"

Then listen. Let them talk. They will usually tell you the situation in their own words — which hospital, what happened, what's going wrong. Don't interrupt the initial download. You're listening for:

- **What hospital** — shapes everything downstream
- **What happened** — admission reason, how long they've been in
- **Who is the patient** vs. who is calling — often a spouse or adult child is calling on behalf
- **What's their most pressing concern right now** — this is the thing you're going to address in Beat 2

If they're scattered (common — they're stressed), gently redirect:

> "OK, let me make sure I understand the situation. [Name] is at [hospital], admitted for [reason], and right now the main thing you're trying to figure out is [X]. Is that right?"

That reflection does two things: it shows you're listening, and it gives them the feeling that their chaotic situation just got organized into something manageable.

### Beat 2 — Address Their #1 Concern (3-4 minutes)

**Goal:** Demonstrate competence by giving them genuinely useful information about the thing they're most worried about right now.

This is the core of the call. You are not selling — you are helping. Common concerns and how to address them:

**"I don't understand what the doctor is recommending"**
- Ask what they've been told so far
- Explain the likely treatment pathway in plain language
- Set expectations: "Typically in this situation, the next step would be [X], which usually takes [Y]"
- If you know the hospital/doctor: "Dr. [name] at [hospital] is [context] — you're in good hands for this particular issue"

**"The bills seem really high / I don't know if I'm being overcharged"**
- Ask what they've been quoted or charged so far
- Give them a benchmark if you can: "For [procedure] at [hospital], we'd typically expect to see [range]"
- Name the specific thing we do: "One of the things we do is audit every charge line by line against our internal benchmarks"

**"Can I fly home for treatment?"**
- Don't dismiss it, don't encourage it
- "That's a question we evaluate case by case. In most situations, stabilization here comes first and evacuation is a Day 2-3 decision. We've managed five medical evacuations and can assess whether that's viable for your case."
- This is a strong hook — it shows we have real capability in an area they're panicking about

**"I can't communicate with the doctors"**
- Acknowledge the gap: "That's one of the most common frustrations — the system here really does assume you speak Indonesian and know how it works"
- Explain what we do: "We act as a communication bridge — we talk to your medical team directly, get the updates, and relay everything to you in plain English"

**"I don't know what my options are"**
- Walk them through the immediate options based on what you know about their case
- If you know the hospital: name the relevant specialists, departments, or alternative facilities
- Frame it: "You actually have more options than you probably realize right now. Let me walk you through what I'd be thinking about in your situation."

**Key principle for Beat 2:** Give them real information. Not generalities, not "we can help with that." Actual, specific, useful guidance based on what they've told you. This is what converts — the experience of talking to someone who knows the system and is willing to share what they know for free.

### Beat 3 — Set Expectations (1-2 minutes)

**Goal:** Tell them what's likely to happen next in their hospital stay so they feel less lost.

Based on what you've learned about their case, give them a brief preview of the next 24-48 hours:

> "So here's what I'd expect to happen next. [Doctor/hospital] will probably [next step]. That usually takes [timeframe]. The thing to watch for is [specific thing]. If [X happens], that's normal. If [Y happens], that's when you'd want to [action]."

This is enormously valuable to someone who feels like they're in a black box. You're giving them a mental map of the near future. Even if you're approximating, the fact that someone can describe what comes next is deeply reassuring.

### Beat 4 — Transition to Professional Engagement (1-2 minutes)

**Goal:** Signal that ongoing support is available, without pressure.

This is the critical beat. The exact framing matters:

> "Look, we can provide further, consistent support if you decide to engage us professionally. We don't charge for this call — right now we're just trying to help. I'm going to send you the advocacy program details over WhatsApp. This probably isn't the right time to read through it all, but when you have a minute, take a look. You can re-engage us at any point in this process."

What this framing accomplishes:
- **"Further, consistent support"** — names what they'd be buying (continuity, not a one-off)
- **"We don't charge for this call"** — makes the free help feel deliberate, not accidental
- **"Right now we're just trying to help"** — positions the call as genuine, not a lead-gen exercise
- **"This probably isn't the right time"** — respects their situation and removes pressure
- **"You can re-engage us at any point"** — the door stays open, they control the timing

**Do not:**
- Quote prices on the call (the document handles that)
- Describe the five risk windows (too much information for a stressed caller)
- Push for a decision ("would you like to get started?")
- Offer a discount or special deal — the pricing is the pricing

### Beat 5 — Close and Follow Up (1 minute)

**Goal:** End warm, send the document.

> "Is there anything else I can help with right now? ... OK. I'll send the program details to this WhatsApp number in just a moment. And [name] — we're here if anything changes or you have more questions. Don't hesitate."

Immediately after hanging up, send via WhatsApp:

> "Hi [name], good talking to you. As discussed, [one-sentence recap of their situation and the main thing you addressed]. Here's the advocacy program overview — no rush, reach out whenever you're ready."

Attach the advocacy program PDF.

## 4. Tone Guide

### 4.1 The Voice

Think: a competent friend who happens to know the healthcare system inside out. Not a doctor (too clinical), not a customer service rep (too scripted), not a counselor (too soft). A knowledgeable person who showed up and said "OK, let me help you figure this out."

### 4.2 Calibration

- **Calm, not slow.** You're unhurried but efficient. Don't drag it out.
- **Direct, not blunt.** Name the reality clearly but without harshness.
- **Confident, not arrogant.** "We've seen this before and here's how it usually goes" — not "we know better than the hospital."
- **Warm, not familiar.** You care, but you just met this person. Don't overdo the empathy.

### 4.3 Things to Never Say

- "I understand how you feel" — you don't, and it sounds hollow
- "Don't worry" — dismissive of real concerns
- "That hospital is terrible" or any criticism of specific facilities/doctors
- "You should have called us sooner" — blaming
- "This is an emergency/crisis" — escalates their anxiety
- "Normally we'd charge for this, but..." — makes the free call feel transactional
- "To be honest..." — implies you're not always honest

### 4.4 Language to Reach For

- "Here's what I'd expect to happen next..."
- "In our experience at [hospital], typically..."
- "You actually have a few options here..."
- "The thing to pay attention to is..."
- "That's a really common frustration — here's what's actually going on..."
- "We're here whenever you're ready."

## 5. Common Scenarios

### 5.1 Caller Is Angry at the Hospital

Listen. Don't agree or disagree with their assessment of the hospital. Redirect to what can be done:

> "I hear you. Let's focus on what we can do about it — here's what I'd suggest as a next step."

Never validate hospital-bashing specifically, even if they're right. Our relationships with these hospitals are what make the service work.

### 5.2 Caller Wants to Engage Immediately on the Call

Great — but still send the document and suggest they review it:

> "Absolutely, we can get started right away. I'm going to send you the program overview so you have everything in writing — the onboarding fee is IDR 1,000,000 and daily coordination is IDR 250,000. I'll have your case manager reach out within [timeframe] to begin the evaluation. Sound good?"

If they're a Padma Care member, note the onboarding waiver.

### 5.3 Caller Is the Patient (Not a Family Member)

Adjust energy — they may be in pain, medicated, or exhausted. Shorter sentences, slower pace, more pauses. Confirm they have someone (family member, friend) who can be a point of contact if they're going into surgery or are heavily medicated. Get that person's WhatsApp number.

### 5.4 Caller Is Skeptical or Price-Shopping

Don't compete. Let the call speak for itself:

> "I'd suggest this — take the information I've given you today and use it however it's helpful. If you decide you want consistent, daily support throughout the rest of this hospital stay, we're here. No pressure either way."

### 5.5 The Situation Is Genuinely Urgent

If the caller describes something that needs immediate medical action (not advocacy — actual medical intervention), direct them first:

> "OK, that sounds like something the medical team needs to handle right now. Here's what I'd tell the nurse: [specific instruction]. Once that's sorted, call me back and we'll work through the rest."

Don't let advocacy onboarding delay necessary medical care.

## 7. Training Notes

### 7.1 Practice the Transition (Beat 4)

The transition from "free help" to "professional engagement available" is the hardest part to get right. Practice it until it feels natural — not scripted, not hesitant. The exact words in Beat 4 are proven to work. Adapt the phrasing to your voice, but keep the structure: acknowledge the free help, name what ongoing support looks like, remove pressure, send the document.

### 7.2 Know Your Hospitals

The more specific you can be in Beat 2 ("at Siloam Denpasar, the orthopedic team usually..."), the more trust you build. This knowledge comes from working cases, debriefing with colleagues, and staying current on which specialists are where. It evolves constantly — new doctors rotate in, departments restructure, pricing shifts. Don't expect to learn it from a document. Sit in on advocacy cases, ask Alex or senior case managers about specific hospitals before calls when possible, and build your own mental map over time. Generic advice sounds generic. Specific advice sounds like expertise.

### 7.3 The Call Is the Product Sample

Repeat this to yourself before every call. You are not describing what Padma does — you are doing it, live, for free, in 10 minutes. If the caller hangs up feeling calmer and better informed than when they called, the document does the rest.
