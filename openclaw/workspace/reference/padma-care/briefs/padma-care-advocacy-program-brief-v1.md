## 1. Document Purpose & Context

### 1.1 What This Document Is

A two-page (front and back, or two separate sheets) PDF that walks a prospective advocacy client through Padma Care's structured approach to managing a hospital case. It introduces the five risk windows, shows what Padma does at each stage, provides a worked pricing example, and includes the full service menu.

This is the companion piece to the patient-facing 1-pager. That document gets them to WhatsApp us. This document gets them to engage professionally.

### 1.2 When It Gets Used

After the initial free check-in call (typically 10 minutes). The call focuses on the caller's most immediate concern — what's happening right now, what their options are, what to expect. The call itself is the demonstration of value. At the end, the conversation goes something like:

> "We can provide further, consistent support if you decide to engage professionally. We don't charge for this call — right now we're just trying to help. I'm going to send you the advocacy program details. This probably isn't the right time to read it all, but when you have a minute, look through it and you can re-engage us at any point in this process."

This means:
- The reader has already spoken with Padma and received genuine help
- They are deciding whether to formalize the engagement
- They may read it immediately, or hours later, or the next morning
- A family member (not the patient) is the most likely reader
- It must be fully self-explanatory — it may be read without a walkthrough

### 1.3 What It Must Do

1. **Introduce the risk window framework** — show that Padma has a structured, proven approach (not ad hoc)
2. **Make the journey legible** — "here's what happens, here's where you are, here's what comes next"
3. **Show pricing clearly** — core engagement cost, worked example, full menu
4. **Reinforce the check-in experience** — "the calm, competent help you just got on the phone continues"
5. **Make re-engagement effortless** — WhatsApp CTA, QR code

### 1.4 What It Must NOT Do

- Overwhelm — this person is already in a hospital situation
- Feel clinical or academic — the risk windows are an operational framework, not a medical textbook
- Introduce new anxieties — "here are all the things that can go wrong" is not the message
- Criticize hospitals, doctors, or the Indonesian system
- Replace the conversation — this supports the relationship, it doesn't substitute for it

## 2. Design Direction

### 2.1 Format

- Two A4 pages (front/back or two sheets)
- Print-ready + digital (WhatsApp-sendable PDF, phone-readable)
- Same Padma Care design family: #505CA0 (blue-purple primary), #FFD4C4 (light salmon secondary), #E6854B (burnt orange CTA accent)

### 2.2 Tone

Same register as the 1-pager: calm, steady, competent. Not a sales pitch — a program description handed to someone by people who have already demonstrated they know what they're doing. Think: the printed version of the voice they heard on the phone.

### 2.3 Visual Approach

- Risk window timeline is the structural backbone of page 1 — top to bottom, with a visual intensity gradient (darker/warmer at top for high-risk, lighter/cooler at bottom for lower-risk)
- Page 1 layout: 2/3 left column (risk window timeline) + 1/3 right column (worked example sidebar)
- Page 2: structured detail table + full service menu + contact
- Clean, airy — adequate white space. The reader is stressed
- Iconography for risk windows (simple, consistent set — not decorative)

## 3. Page 1 — The Journey

### 3.1 Header

**Padma Medical Group logo** (top, consistent with collateral family)

**Sub-brand line:**

> PADMA CARE — Patient Advocacy Program

### 3.2 Page Title

> Your case. Our structure.

Design note: Short, confident, non-dramatic. Signals "we have a system for this." Alternative: "How we manage your hospital case" — more literal, less punchy.

### 3.3 Brief Introductory Paragraph

> Every hospital case follows a pattern. Over 1,900 patients and 7 hospital partnerships, we've developed a structured approach to managing yours — from the moment we engage through discharge and beyond. Here's what that looks like.

Design note: Two sentences. Establishes experience and structure. Then gets out of the way.

### 3.4 Risk Windows — Timeline (Left 2/3 Column)

Five blocks, top to bottom. Each block contains:
- **Window number and name** (bold)
- **Engagement tag** (top-right corner of each block): `REQUIRED` / `RECOMMENDED` / `AT YOUR PREFERENCE`
- **1-2 sentence summary** of what's happening medically
- **1-2 sentence summary** of what Padma does
- **Transition trigger** — what signals the move to the next window

Visual treatment: decreasing color intensity from Window 1 (darkest/warmest) to Window 5 (lightest/coolest). The gradient communicates decreasing risk without saying "risk" repeatedly.

---

**Window 1 — Triage & Stabilization** `REQUIRED`

> **What's happening:** The hospital emergency team stabilizes the patient. Once stabilization allows, decisions start: procedure planning, surgeon and specialist selection, location, timing. This is the highest-risk moment — decisions are made with limited information and under time pressure.

> **What Padma does:** We evaluate the situation, coordinate a surgical conference, present options clearly to you and your family, help select the best option for your case, and set expectations for what comes next. We also evaluate whether medical evacuation is viable and will raise it proactively if it's medically appropriate.

> **Note:** In acute emergency situations, the hospital's emergency team leads. Our role begins once initial stabilization allows for collaborative care planning.

> **Ends when:** Treatment plan is confirmed, procedure is scheduled.

---

**Window 2 — Procedure** `REQUIRED`

> **What's happening:** Surgery or primary medical intervention. Risks vary by procedure; residual risks from general anesthesia are present in most surgical cases.

> **What Padma does:** We screen for anesthesia risks based on your medical and surgical history — allergies, prior reactions, conditions that warrant extra caution. This pre-operative review is a consistent gap in standard pre-op protocols that we actively remediate. We receive a verbal post-surgical update and reconcile it against the eventual medical records.

> **Ends when:** Patient is moved to inpatient room post-procedure.

---

**Window 3 — Primary Recovery** `REQUIRED`

> **What's happening:** Post-surgical recovery (or post-final-surgery, in multi-intervention cases). The team monitors for complications — infections, improper healing, side effects, surgical errors. This period is typically 2–10 days and ends when the patient transitions off IV medications.

> **What Padma does:** Constant check-ins beyond the single daily doctor visit. We want better and more frequent information on recovery than the standard protocol provides. This is also where quality-of-life supports are most valuable: private nursing, catering (recovery-appropriate meals), wifi setup, trips home for personal items.

> **Ends when:** IV lines are removed and patient is cleared for discharge planning. Patients cannot be discharged prior to IV line removal.

---

**Window 4 — Discharge & Transition** `RECOMMENDED`

> **What's happening:** Discharge takes many forms. Some patients stay longer for observation (sometimes medically necessary), others want to leave as soon as possible. A third option: a supervised medical villa environment with full-time nurse, chef, and recovery amenities — out of the hospital but still under managed care.

> **What Padma does:** Daily check-ins, available for any questions, coordinating follow-up appointments to prevent the haphazard scheduling that otherwise happens. This phase typically concludes with incision closure and the first kontrol (post-operative follow-up) visit. We also require one billable day to review all charges against our internal pricing benchmarks and prepare the comprehensive English discharge report. We can arrange post-discharge transport (ambulance or large SUV) to ensure smooth transition with no medical regression.

> **Ends when:** First kontrol visit is complete, discharge report is delivered.

---

**Window 5 — Rehabilitation & Post-Op** `AT YOUR PREFERENCE`

> **What's happening:** Physical therapy, rehabilitation, and post-operative kontrol appointments. The acute phase is over but recovery continues.

> **What Padma does:** We continue daily check-ins, track progress toward rehabilitation milestones, coordinate kontrol appointments, and remain available for questions or concerns. We stay involved to the extent you prefer — some patients want daily contact through full recovery, others are comfortable managing independently after discharge.

> **Ends when:** You tell us you're good.

### 4.2 Risk Window Detail Table

A structured table (or structured layout — designer's discretion on exact format) with one row per risk window and the following columns:

| Column | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Window** | Number, name, and engagement tag (Required / Recommended / At Your Preference) |
| **Medical Activity** | What is typically happening clinically |
| **Key Risks** | What can go wrong or what requires vigilance (framed as "what we watch for," not fear-mongering) |
| **Padma Care Intervention** | Specific actions we take |
| **Endpoint** | What triggers transition to the next window |

**Window 1 — Triage & Stabilization** `REQUIRED`
- **Medical Activity:** Emergency stabilization, diagnostic evaluation, procedure planning, surgeon/specialist selection, surgical conference
- **Key Risks:** Decisions made under time pressure with limited information. Bias toward whoever is "in the room." ER influence is limited — hospital emergency team leads acute care
- **PC Intervention:** Case evaluation, surgical conference coordination, options presentation to patient/family, evacuation viability assessment, specialist selection guidance
- **Endpoint:** Treatment plan confirmed, procedure scheduled

**Window 2 — Procedure** `REQUIRED`
- **Medical Activity:** Surgery or primary intervention, anesthesia administration, post-surgical stabilization
- **Key Risks:** Procedure-specific risks, general anesthesia risks (prior reactions, allergies, contraindications)
- **PC Intervention:** Pre-op anesthesia risk screening from patient history, monitoring for gaps in standard pre-op protocol, post-surgical verbal update with records reconciliation
- **Endpoint:** Patient moved to inpatient room

**Window 3 — Primary Recovery** `REQUIRED`
- **Medical Activity:** Post-surgical monitoring, IV medication management, wound care, mobility assessment
- **Key Risks:** Infection, improper healing, medication side effects, surgical complications. Standard protocol provides one doctor visit per day — insufficient for active monitoring
- **PC Intervention:** Multiple daily check-ins, active complication monitoring, quality-of-life support coordination (nursing, catering, wifi, personal errands), communication bridge with medical team
- **Endpoint:** IV lines removed, discharge planning begins

**Window 4 — Discharge & Transition** `RECOMMENDED`
- **Medical Activity:** Wound closure, mobility clearance, discharge assessment, first kontrol (post-op follow-up) scheduling
- **Key Risks:** Haphazard follow-up scheduling, billing errors, medical regression during transfer, premature discharge
- **PC Intervention:** Daily check-ins, follow-up appointment coordination, comprehensive bill audit against internal benchmarks, English discharge report preparation, transport arrangement (ambulance/SUV), medical villa referral if appropriate
- **Endpoint:** First kontrol visit complete, discharge report delivered

**Window 5 — Rehabilitation & Post-Op** `AT YOUR PREFERENCE`
- **Medical Activity:** Physical therapy, rehabilitation milestones, kontrol appointments, medication tapering
- **Key Risks:** Incomplete rehabilitation, missed follow-up appointments, undetected complications post-discharge
- **PC Intervention:** Daily check-ins (at patient preference), rehabilitation progress tracking, kontrol appointment coordination, ongoing availability for questions
- **Endpoint:** Patient-determined

## 5. Messaging Principles (Program Walkthrough-Specific)

These supplement the principles in the patient-facing 1-pager brief (Section 4) and the concierge brochure brief (Section 5).

### 5.1 Core Message

"We have a system for this." The reader should finish page 1 feeling that their chaotic, unpredictable situation maps to something Padma has seen before and has a structured approach to managing. Not "we've seen it all" (arrogant) — "we have a framework that adapts to your situation" (competent).

### 5.2 The Tags Do the Work

The `REQUIRED` / `RECOMMENDED` / `AT YOUR PREFERENCE` tags communicate the engagement structure without lengthy explanations. They're honest — we don't pretend that Window 5 is as critical as Window 1 — and they respect the patient's autonomy by making clear where choice exists.

### 5.3 Risk Language

Name what Padma watches for, not what the patient should fear. "We monitor for post-surgical complications including infection and improper healing" is professional. "Infections are common and can be life-threatening" is alarming. The framing is always "here's what we're paying attention to" — it positions Padma as vigilant without making the reader more anxious.

### 5.4 Pricing Transparency

Show real numbers. The worked example exists because "IDR 250,000/day" is abstract — "IDR 6,600,000 total for a 12-day surgical case" is concrete. Currency conversions acknowledge that the reader thinks in their home currency. The "every case is different" note prevents the example from becoming a binding quote.

### 5.5 Do

- Frame the five windows as a journey with a beginning, middle, and end
- Use the tags consistently — they become a shared vocabulary with the patient
- Show that Padma fills specific, named gaps in the standard hospital experience
- Acknowledge that the reader's family member may be the decision-maker
- Make re-engagement feel like resuming a conversation, not starting a transaction

### 5.6 Don't

- Use the word "risk" in headlines or window titles — it's in the internal framework name, not the patient-facing language
- List every possible complication — name categories, not exhaustive lists
- Make the medical villa sound like a luxury hotel — it's a clinical recovery environment that happens to be comfortable
- Over-explain the engagement structure — the tags and the worked example do most of the work

## 6. Distribution Notes

### 6.1 Primary Channel

Sent via WhatsApp as a PDF immediately after the free check-in call. The send message should be brief:

> "Here's the advocacy program overview I mentioned. No rush — take a look when you have a moment and reach out anytime."

### 6.2 Secondary Channels

- Shared in person with prospective clients met outside the hospital context
- Shared with referring doctors who want to understand the full program (not just the 1-pager they hand to patients)

### 6.3 Technical Requirements

- WhatsApp-optimized file size (compress images, reasonable resolution)
- Readable on phone screen — test at phone dimensions, not just A4
- Printable on standard office printer for in-person distribution
- Two-page PDF, not a four-page booklet — keep it flat
